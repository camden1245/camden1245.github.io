Why this work

Most of what follows is hardware I specified, built and then had to debug. A spin-coating platform that drives a syringe pump and a closed-loop BLDC head in step, where the firmware was setting speed from a motor rating that turned out to be wrong by as much as 4,700 RPM. A resonant thin-film sensor whose analog front end was simulated as an equivalent circuit before any topology was chosen, and whose thermal design was settled by comparing five mounting configurations two independent ways. The rest of this page is semiconductor devices built by hand and the metrology used to decide whether to believe them. The thread running through all of it: in three of these projects the first answer the instrument gave was wrong, and the real work was finding out why.

Designing Out Thermal Drift in a Resonant Thin-Film Sensor

Waterloo Hacker Fab — DC magnetron sputtering system · Spring 2026 – present

ProblemThe sputtering system needs to know how thick a film is while it is growing. A quartz crystal microbalance infers deposited mass from a shift in the crystal's resonant frequency, which means anything else that changes the resonator — including radiant heat from the sputter head — is indistinguishable from deposition. A QCM that drifts does not fail visibly; it returns a confident wrong number. The mount had to be chosen before the sensor was built, because the mount is what sets how much of that heat reaches the crystal.

ApproachI built a transient lumped-parameter thermal model in Python covering five candidate mounting configurations, from a bare floating crystal to a water-cooled holder. Incident flux from the plasma was taken as 0.03 W/cm², and effective emissivity — by far the least certain input — was swept from 0.05 to 0.75 rather than assumed. I then built an independent 2-D axisymmetric finite-element model in COMSOL from the SolidWorks enclosure geometry and ran four of the configurations again, as a check on the lumped model rather than a repeat of it.

ResultA floating crystal settles near 108 °C; light edge clamps hold it at 40 °C for a small conductive penalty, which is the configuration selected for the build. The two models agree to 0.4 °C on the clamped case and 1.6 °C on the floating case. They disagree by 4 °C on the sandwich-plate case, and the passive-mount FEA run was stopped at 15 s before it reached steady state — both are open, and both are shown below rather than left out. A smaller correction mattered too: a 6 MHz AT-cut crystal is 0.277 mm thick, not the 0.33 mm commonly quoted, and the thermal mass follows from that.

Thermal Drift, Continued — What the Design Is Actually Sensitive To

Waterloo Hacker Fab — three-dimensional COMSOL parametric study · September 2026

QuestionChoosing a mount answered one question and raised another: of all the numbers that went into that model, which ones does the answer actually depend on? Two of them are still open to whoever builds the thing. The surface finish of the aluminium housing sets how readily it radiates heat away, and the conductance across the crystal-to-holder interface is the least certain input in the model, being a fitted quantity rather than a datasheet value.

ApproachI rebuilt the enclosure as a full three-dimensional COMSOL model from the SolidWorks geometry, in place of the earlier axisymmetric slice, and swept both inputs: aluminium emissivity from 0.05 to 0.8, which spans bare polished aluminium at one end and an anodised finish at the other, and contact conductance from 200 to 5,000 W/(m²·K).

ResultEmissivity decides the design and nothing else comes close. Housing temperature falls from 122 °C at ε = 0.05 to 83 °C at 0.1, 46 °C at 0.3, and 31 °C at 0.8: a 91 °C span bought with a surface treatment. Most of it arrives early, since reaching ε = 0.3 removes 76 °C and the entire remaining range is worth another 15. Contact conductance, the input I was least sure of, turned out to matter least. A factor of twenty-five increase moves the housing by a few degrees at ε = 0.1 and by essentially nothing at 0.3, which is a useful thing to know before building, because it means the design does not rest on controlling an interface that is hard to control. The crystal tracks the housing closely, sitting about 0.9 °C above it, so housing temperature is the quantity worth designing against.

In measurement unitsConverted into what the instrument would actually report, the drift stays below 0.3 nm of apparent aluminium through the first 5,000 s and reaches roughly 2.6 nm over a four-hour run. Short depositions are not thermally limited at all; only long ones need the compensation path built. The instruction this study produces is a finish specification rather than a mechanism: anodise the housing.

ToolsCOMSOL Multiphysics (three-dimensional, parametric sweep) · SolidWorks · Python

Building and Testing an Automated Layer-by-Layer Spin Coater

McGill University, Soft Matter Lab · Jan–Apr 2025; Sept 2025–Apr 2026 · Supervisor: Dr. Reghan J. Hill

ProblemThe group's layer-by-layer deposition was done by hand, so repeatability was limited by whoever was doing it. It needed an instrument that produced the same film twice — and then it needed evidence that the films it produced were real, uniform, and electrically measurable.

What I built

A full deposition platform for two-solution alternating layer-by-layer coating, specified and assembled from components, with the firmware written from scratch. One run automates stage motion, alternating solution positioning, syringe-pump triggering, spin-speed control and process timing — the five things that had previously depended on the operator.

Controller
Arduino Uno R3, with an OLED display for status and cycle progress
Motion
Linear stage driven by a TB6600 stepper driver, with a limit switch for homing
Spin head
BLDC motor and ESC on the spin platform
Dispensing
New Era syringe pump, TTL-triggered, with micro-tubing and dispensing tips
Power
12 V supply in a dedicated electronics enclosure
Firmware
Arduino C/C++ — main control logic, OLED screen logic, and icon data in PROGMEM

A run homes the stage against the limit switch, moves to the neutral position, primes the pump with pulsed triggering, then for each layer steps to solution position A or B, ramps the motor to deposition speed, pulses the pump while the substrate spins, returns to neutral, lets the droplet spread, ramps to a higher speed to drive off excess, and ramps back down. Positions A and B alternate to build the multilayer, and the stage re-homes periodically to correct drift. Substrates were copper discs polished to roughly a 0.3 µm finish; reused discs went through an ethanol sonication and a brief acidified-peroxide oxide strip before coating.

Calibrating the drive — the number the firmware trusted was wrong

The firmware initially set speed from the motor's Kv rating, so before running any films I checked it: I swept the full range against a tachometer and recorded actual rotational speed, loaded and unloaded. The Kv rating was not the speed. Measured rotation ran between 1,630 and 4,720 RPM above what the rating predicted, and the error was not a constant offset or a constant factor — it grew toward mid-range and then closed again, so nothing simple would have corrected it. I fitted the measured curve and inverted that fit in firmware, so a commanded 5,000 RPM now turns at 5,000 RPM and every process parameter in the recipe is a real speed rather than a nominal one. The rig's measured ceiling is roughly 8,900 RPM unloaded.

Finding the process window

With the drive calibrated I established the process window by structured sweeps over speed, dispense volume and drying. The settled protocol is 0.45 mL per deposition step, 3,000 RPM for 30 s to form the coating, then 6,500 RPM for 8 s to remove excess and stabilise drying — the same base recipe for both the chitosan and xanthan gum steps. An earlier recipe of 4,000 RPM for 25 s followed by 6,500 RPM for 20 s left finger-like drying patterns across the film; the settled recipe removed them. The solutions themselves took their own optimization: chitosan at 0.70% w/v in 1% w/v acetic acid and xanthan gum at 0.35% w/v in RO water, concentrations chosen for a viscosity range that spin-coats into a stable film without agglomeration.

Long runs needed reliability features that only show up once you run hundreds of cycles: re-homing every 20 cycles, pulsed pump triggering rather than one long trigger, gradual motor ramps instead of abrupt steps, and staying out of the low nonzero RPM band where the motor runs unstably. Films were built at 10, 30, 50, 100, 150 and 250 layers.

Confirming there was a film at all

Two independent early checks, before any of the more involved metrology. Contact angle showed that wetting changed measurably against bare copper, so the surface had been modified. Weighing the substrate before and after deposition showed mass increasing with layer count out to 250 layers, which is the evidence that the platform accumulates material repeatably rather than depositing and stripping in equal measure.

Mixing the two solutions directly gave the reason the pair works: chitosan and xanthan gum form a visible complex polymer network, which is what makes them a viable alternating polycation/polyanion pair for layer-by-layer growth.

Structure, coverage and composition

SEM covered film coverage, cross-sectional thickness and the edge profile against the substrate. Early films showed non-uniform regions tied to copper surface roughness and substrate condition, which is what pushed the later work on substrate preparation. SEM-EDS separated well-coated from poorly coated regions: well-coated areas showed the elevated carbon and oxygen expected of an organic CT/XG film with the copper signal suppressed, while spectra from poorly coated regions showed the substrate coming through, mapping coverage variation spatially rather than by eye.

Stylus profilometry gave the thickness ground truth and the surface topography. A 100-layer film measured 1.766 µm against its reference scan, which puts growth at roughly 11–18 nm per bilayer across the 30- and 100-layer stacks, consistent with published values for the same system. The scans put average roughness at Sa = 2.49 nm and Sq = 4.22 nm.

Electrical behaviour of the films

Impedance spectroscopy on a Keysight E4990A from 20 Hz to 10 MHz, with samples mounted in a coin-cell style compression fixture with stainless electrodes. Coated substrates showed higher real impedance than bare copper — an added resistive barrier — and a stronger imaginary response, a capacitive and dielectric contribution.

Layer count moves both components, and it moves them by orders of magnitude rather than marginally — which is what connects deposition history to electrical behaviour, and what makes the number of layers a process knob rather than just a count.

Converting the compensated impedance into material quantities gives complex conductivity and complex relative permittivity, which is where the film's own behaviour shows rather than the fixture's. For the soaked film, σ′ rises steadily with frequency across the full band, from below 0.5 × 10−4 S m−1 at the low end to about 5 × 10−4 S m−1 approaching 108 rad s−1.

Permittivity is where hydration state shows most clearly. Dry films, films held in an 80% RH chamber and films soaked 10 minutes in RO water separate into three distinct curves, with the soaked film sitting orders of magnitude above the dry one at low frequency — water raises ionic mobility and changes how charge is stored and released at the interfaces. That is what pushed the soak-stability work below: a spectrum that moves this much with water is only meaningful if the film itself is still intact.

Separating the film from the instrument

The harder half of the electrical work was deciding which features belonged to the film and which to the measurement. I built and measured known RC circuits — a 10 kΩ + 10 nF series combination among them — as benchmarks, which exposed where the analyser and fixture stop behaving: real impedance holds near the expected resistor value across a central band, with deviations appearing at high frequency and, depending on the R and C values, at low frequency too. I then compared correction approaches on those known circuits: raw, open-short compensation in several formulations, and handbook-style open-short-load, scored by median percent error against the expected value.

Alongside that I worked through the fixture itself: grounded Faraday enclosure, twisted leads and reduced loop area, keeping open, short and load at the same effective measurement plane, and minimising contact variability in the clips. The practical rule that came out of it is that compensation is only trustworthy when open, short and load are taken at the same true measurement plane — small changes in lead geometry or contact location make an aggressive correction unstable, and the more aggressive corrections were exactly the ones most sensitive to it.

Stability in water

If the films dissolved when wet, the hydration-dependent impedance results would mean something different than if they took up water and stayed intact. Soaking a 100-layer CT/XG film in 200 mL of RO water and tracking bulk conductivity every minute settled it, with single-component films as controls.

Where it ended up

The platform runs multi-cycle CT/XG depositions on its own, with every process parameter in the recipe a measured quantity rather than a nominal one, and the procedures written up as a method another person can run. The films are confirmed by mass, contact angle, SEM/EDS and profilometry, and they have a measurable electrical signature that tracks both layer count and hydration state. The part I would defend hardest is the measurement work: most of the recent effort went into telling true material response apart from instrument limits, fixture parasitics and compensation artifacts, because a number you cannot attribute is not a result.

Cross-Validating a Thin-Film Thickness Measurement

Course project — NE 343 Microfabrication & Thin-Film Technology · Spring 2026

ProblemI measured the same PECVD silicon nitride film three ways and got three answers, spanning nearly a factor of two. Any one of them, reported alone, would have been taken as the thickness — and everything downstream, including the dielectric constant of the film, scales directly with whichever one you believe.

ApproachOptical colour chart, DEKTAK stylus profilometry and spectroscopic ellipsometry disagreed. Rather than average them, I looked for evidence that did not come from any of the three. Two independent checks were available: the film's mass, weighed on the wafer before and after deposition, and the maximum capacitance of MIS devices built on the same film.

ResultThe ellipsometric value of 1000 Å is the one the independent evidence supports. At that thickness the measured 1.2 mg of film over the wafer area gives a density of 2.63 g/cm³, inside the 2.5–3.1 g/cm³ range reported for PECVD nitride; at 1800 Å it gives 1.46 g/cm³, below any published value for the material. The electrical check agrees: 1000 Å yields εr = 6.2, within the usual 6–9 for PECVD SiNx, while 1800 Å would require 11.2. The profilometer was not wrong so much as answering a different question — RIE has finite nitride-to-silicon selectivity, so the exposed silicon was etched below the original surface and the measured step contains both the film and the recess beneath it. The useful outcome is not the number but the rule: a stylus measures a step, not a film, and the two are only the same when the etch stops where you think it did.

ToolsSpectroscopic ellipsometry (Woollam alpha-SE) · DEKTAK stylus profilometry · reactive ion etch · gravimetric analysis

MIS Capacitor — Full Fabrication Flow and Electrical Test

Course project — NE 343 Microfabrication & Thin-Film Technology · Spring 2026

ProblemBuild an Al/SiNx/p-Si metal–insulator–semiconductor capacitor from a bare wafer across three cleanroom sessions, and determine from its electrical behaviour what the dielectric and the interface are actually doing.

ApproachComplete flow: RCA clean, PECVD silicon nitride, contact photolithography, reactive ion etch, DC magnetron sputtered aluminium, wet etch. Then C–V at 1 MHz and I–V on nine devices in a cross-pattern, across five contact diameters and five wafer positions, so that area effects and spatial variation could be separated.

ResultCmax against contact area is linear to R² ≈ 1.00, giving εr = 6.2. From Cmax = 39.8 pF and Cmin = 15.6 pF the substrate doping works out to Na ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, which lands inside the wafer's specified resistivity range — an independent confirmation that the extraction is sound. The interesting part is where the device is not ideal. Flatband sits near −20 V against an ideal −0.68 V, a shift of −19.3 V corresponding to a positive fixed charge density of 6.8 × 10¹² cm⁻² in the nitride, which is what SiH4/NH3 PECVD film is expected to carry. Capacitance stayed within ±3% across the wafer and leakage below 10⁻¹⁰ A out to 20 V with no breakdown: a film with a lot of fixed charge, but a spatially uniform and electrically robust one. What stayed with me was how tightly the steps couple — what you accept at develop, you pay for at etch, and then again at extraction.

ToolsPECVD · DC magnetron sputtering · photolithography · reactive ion etch · C–V and I–V characterisation · four-point probe

Quantum-Dot LED from Synthesised Material

Course project — Nanomaterials Synthesis Laboratory · Spring 2026

ProblemTake a nanomaterial from precursors to a device that emits light, and show that the structural measurement and the optical behaviour agree.

ApproachSynthesised CdSe quantum dots, SPIONs, and europium- and dysprosium-doped phosphors, then built a spin-cast quantum-dot LED from the CdSe. Emission was tuned by controlling growth time alone, and I correlated image-derived size distributions against measured emission using the Brus model, with magnetic response treated through Néel and Brownian relaxation.

ResultClosing the loop from particle size to device output is the part I care about — a spectrum on its own does not tell you whether you understand the material.
